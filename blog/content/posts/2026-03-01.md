**Frontier LLMs still fail badly on long, multi-turn workflows**

- Research led by Philippe Laban shows that state-of-the-art LLMs “from GPT‑5 onward” lose up to 33% accuracy when required information is distributed across multiple chat turns (“sharded”) instead of a single concatenated prompt, across six task types spanning code, databases, tool actions, data-to-text, math, and summarization ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/even-frontier-llms-from-gpt-5-onward-lose-up-to-33-accuracy-when-you-chat-too-long/)).

- The degradation has only modestly improved over generations (from ~39% to ~33%), with some Python tasks seeing smaller 10–20% drops, and lowering sampling temperature does not fix the problem, indicating a structural limitation in multi-turn reasoning rather than mere randomness ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/even-frontier-llms-from-gpt-5-onward-lose-up-to-33-accuracy-when-you-chat-too-long/)).

- The team recommends periodically resetting long chats by having the model summarize all outstanding requests and using that summary as the initial prompt in a fresh conversation, warning that real users who change their minds mid-thread likely incur even steeper accuracy losses than seen in simple user simulations ([The Decoder](https://the-decoder.com/even-frontier-llms-from-gpt-5-onward-lose-up-to-33-accuracy-when-you-chat-too-long/)).

**X’s incentive structure supercharges war disinformation about Iran strikes**

- After Donald Trump announced a joint US–Israeli “major combat operation” against Iran, X rapidly filled with misleading viral posts about the strikes and Tehran’s response, many of which WIRED verified as months- or years-old videos, AI-generated images, or even video-game footage misrepresented as live conflict scenes ([WIRED: disinformation on X](https://www.wired.com/story/x-is-drowning-in-disinformation-following-us-and-israels-attack-on-iran/)).

- Nearly all of the most viral misleading posts reviewed came from blue-check accounts that pay for X Premium and can earn revenue from engagement, and although some were later tagged with corrective Community Notes, they remained online and had often already amassed millions of views before corrections appeared ([WIRED: disinformation on X](https://www.wired.com/story/x-is-drowning-in-disinformation-following-us-and-israels-attack-on-iran/)).

- Examples included a 4.4-million-view clip falsely labeled as Iranian missiles over Dubai that actually showed Iranian strikes on Tel Aviv in October 2024, an unverified video of an allegedly downed Israeli jet despite no credible reports of such an incident, and a supposed “hypersonic missile” hit on Haifa that was actually July footage of an Israeli strike on Syria’s defense ministry in Damascus ([WIRED: disinformation on X](https://www.wired.com/story/x-is-drowning-in-disinformation-following-us-and-israels-attack-on-iran/)).

- Pro-Iran and pro-Trump accounts also spread false imagery, such as a Dubai photo mislabeled as an “IRANIAN MISSILE IMPACT IN TEL AVIV” and “before/after” shots of Ali Khamenei’s palace where the “before” image was actually the Mausoleum of Ruhollah Khomeini, showing how both state-aligned media and partisan influencers are leveraging X’s reach and monetization in the fog of war ([WIRED: disinformation on X](https://www.wired.com/story/x-is-drowning-in-disinformation-following-us-and-israels-attack-on-iran/)).

**Hacked Iranian prayer app and blackouts highlight cyber and information warfare**

- As US and Israeli “preemptive” strikes hit Iran, users of the BadeSaba Calendar prayer-times app—downloaded more than 5 million times from Google Play—received a rapid sequence of hacked push notifications urging Iranian military personnel to surrender, join unnamed “forces of liberation,” and promising amnesty for those who lay down their weapons ([WIRED Middle East: hacked prayer app](https://www.wired.com/story/hacked-prayer-app-sends-surrender-messages-to-iranians-amid-israeli-strikes/)).

- Cybersecurity analysts say no group has claimed responsibility and attribution is currently unknown, but they note that compromising such a widely used app and timing the messages to coincide with kinetic strikes suggests a preplanned, likely nation-state–grade operation rather than a quick opportunistic hack ([WIRED Middle East: hacked prayer app](https://www.wired.com/story/hacked-prayer-app-sends-surrender-messages-to-iranians-amid-israeli-strikes/)).

- In parallel with these information operations, Iran launched retaliatory missile attacks on US-linked bases in Bahrain, Kuwait, the UAE, and Qatar, while domestically the country saw a drastic internet disruption, with NetBlocks estimating overall network traffic dropping to about 4% of normal and Iranian cloud-provider data showing major data centers losing international connectivity ([WIRED Middle East: hacked prayer app](https://www.wired.com/story/hacked-prayer-app-sends-surrender-messages-to-iranians-amid-israeli-strikes/)).

- Phone lines, SMS, mobile data, fixed broadband, and even VPN services have been severely degraded, and state-affiliated outlets IRNA and ISNA reportedly suffered cyberattacks that took their sites offline—IRNA has since been restored but ISNA remained down at publication—reviving public fears rooted in earlier 2025 protest crackdowns, during which the government (by its own statistics) recorded at least 3,117 civilian deaths amid prolonged digital blackouts ([WIRED Middle East: hacked prayer app](https://www.wired.com/story/hacked-prayer-app-sends-surrender-messages-to-iranians-amid-israeli-strikes/)).

**UAE uses THAAD/Patriot shield to intercept Iranian missiles, with deadly debris**

- Following Iran’s retaliatory ballistic-missile strikes on US military bases across the Gulf, the UAE Ministry of Defense reported that its air defense systems intercepted several incoming missiles before they reached targets, but debris from one interception fell in Abu Dhabi and killed a civilian near strategic sites including Al Dhafra Air Base, which hosts Emirati and US forces ([WIRED Middle East: UAE missile defense](https://www.wired.com/story/uae-missile-intercept-system-iran/)).

- The UAE’s layered missile-defense architecture employs high-altitude Terminal High Altitude Area Defense (THAAD) batteries—acquired in 2015 and first used operationally in 2022—to destroy ballistic missiles via hit-to-kill kinetic impact in the upper atmosphere, supplemented by lower-altitude MIM‑104 Patriot systems for additional intercept opportunities ([WIRED Middle East: UAE missile defense](https://www.wired.com/story/uae-missile-intercept-system-iran/)).

- These systems are cued by long-range AN/TPY‑2 X-band radars capable of detecting and tracking small, fast-moving objects hundreds of kilometers away, but even when intercepts succeed against missiles traveling over 20,000 km/h, resulting fragments can still pose lethal risks on the ground—as illustrated by Saturday’s fatal debris incident in Abu Dhabi ([WIRED Middle East: UAE missile defense](https://www.wired.com/story/uae-missile-intercept-system-iran/)).

**NASA overhauls Artemis: cancels SLS Block 1B, defers first landing to Artemis IV**

- NASA Administrator Jared Isaacman announced a sweeping restructuring of the Artemis program that cancels the Exploration Upper Stage and Block 1B upgrade for the Space Launch System (SLS), aiming instead to standardize SLS in a single configuration to reduce complexity, fix hydrogen/helium leak–driven delays, and increase flight cadence ([WIRED: Artemis changes](https://www.wired.com/story/nasa-is-making-big-changes-to-speed-up-the-artemis-program/)).

- Artemis II and III will now use the existing SLS upper stage, with Artemis III—targeted for mid‑2027—recast from a first lunar landing attempt into a mission where Orion launches on SLS and docks in low Earth orbit with SpaceX’s Starship and/or Blue Origin’s Blue Moon landers, making Artemis IV the new first crewed landing mission ([WIRED: Artemis changes](https://www.wired.com/story/nasa-is-making-big-changes-to-speed-up-the-artemis-program/)).

- NASA’s goal is to move from launching SLS roughly once every 3.5 years to approximately annual Artemis missions starting with Artemis III, flying SLS through at least Artemis V (and possibly longer) until commercial alternatives can deliver crews to lunar orbit or the surface, while contractors including Boeing have signaled at least tentative support despite losing the multibillion-dollar Exploration Upper Stage contract ([WIRED: Artemis changes](https://www.wired.com/story/nasa-is-making-big-changes-to-speed-up-the-artemis-program/)).

- With only two Delta IV–derived upper stages left and Block 1B canceled, NASA will procure a new human-rated “standardized” upper stage—industry observers point to ULA’s Centaur V as the likely choice—and the shift raises major questions about the fate of the costly new SLS launch tower and the Lunar Gateway station, potentially redirecting funds and focus toward an eventual lunar surface base program ([WIRED: Artemis changes](https://www.wired.com/story/nasa-is-making-big-changes-to-speed-up-the-artemis-program/)).

**Xiaomi 17 Ultra and Leica Leitzphone double down on camera-first flagships**

- Xiaomi’s new 17 Ultra refines last year’s 15 Ultra rather than radically redesigning it and is joined by a Leica co-branded Leitzphone variant that emphasizes photography with Leica-inspired minimalist industrial design, a ribbed aluminum alloy frame for grip, a rotatable ring around the camera for fine zoom control, and a Leica-themed user interface overhaul ([WIRED: Xiaomi 17 Ultra & Leitzphone review](https://www.wired.com/review/xiaomi-17-ultra-leitzphone/)).

- The Leitzphone supports an optional photography kit that adds a dedicated shutter button and other camera-focused ergonomics, and while Xiaomi—now the world’s third-largest phone maker—positions the 17 Ultra line as a top-tier camera phone, it is still not officially sold in the US, leaving American enthusiasts to import at their own risk ([WIRED: Xiaomi 17 Ultra & Leitzphone review](https://www.wired.com/review/xiaomi-17-ultra-leitzphone/)).
